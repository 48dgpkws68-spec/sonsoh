How to choose sleep earbuds for side sleepers

The short answer: the best sleep earbuds for side sleepers sit flush inside the outer ear with nothing sticking out, weigh a few grams, come with several tip sizes and play masking sound at low volume with an auto-off timer. Comfort under pillow pressure matters more than audio specs: if you feel them lying down, they are the wrong earbuds.

By Nick van Straaten · Updated 24 November 2026

What matters

Five things decide comfort on your side.

Profile

Anything that sits proud of the ear gets pushed into it by the pillow. Look for a housing that ends level with the outer ear.

Weight

Every gram is a gram you can feel. A light earbud also stays put when you turn over.

Tips

The tip seals the ear and carries most of the comfort. You want several sizes in soft silicone or foam.

Sound

Blocking, masking or cancelling. Know which one your noise needs before you buy.

Power

Earplugs need nothing. Earbuds need enough battery for your night and a timer that switches them off.

In detail

The criteria, one by one.

1. Profile: the pillow decides

When you lie on your side, the pillow presses on your outer ear with part of the weight of your head. Anything that stands out from the ear, such as a stem, a wing tip or a large round housing, becomes the pressure point. That is why many everyday earbuds feel fine when you sit up and sore once you lie on them.

So look at an earbud from the side, not from the front. The real question is how far it sticks out beyond the outer ear once it is in. Flush-fit sleep earbuds are shaped to sit inside the concha, the bowl of the outer ear, so the pillow rests on your ear instead of on the device.

2. Size and weight

Smaller and lighter is better, within reason: an earbud still needs room for a battery that lasts the night. A light earbud also shifts less when you turn over, which helps it stay where it should. When you compare models, look at the weight per earbud rather than the weight of the case. For reference, SONSOH NIGHT is specified at 2.9 grams per earbud.

3. Ear tips and fit

The ear tip does two jobs. It holds the earbud in place, and it seals the ear canal, which is where most of the passive noise reduction comes from. Ears differ from person to person and often from left to right, so a choice of sizes matters more than any single clever shape.

  • Silicone tips are easy to clean and keep their shape. Dual-flange tips have two layers that help them seal and stay put.
  • Memory foam tips expand to fill the ear canal and can block a little more sound, but they wear out sooner and need replacing more often.
  • Pick the smallest size that still seals. A tip that is too large creates pressure when you lie on it; one that is too small works loose during the night.
  • Keep them clean. Wipe the tips regularly and let them dry before you put them back in.

4. Blocking, masking or cancelling

Sleep audio deals with noise in three different ways, and each one suits a different kind of noise.

  • Passive blocking: the seal of the tip muffles sound. It works on everything and needs no power, but no seal fully stops a loud snorer.
  • Masking: a steady, soft sound such as rain or brown noise makes sudden or irregular sounds, like snoring, traffic or a closing door, stand out less. It works best at low volume on top of a good seal.
  • Active noise cancelling (ANC): microphones and processing cancel steady, low drones such as an engine or air conditioning. It uses extra battery, so check how long a model lasts with ANC switched on.

For a snoring partner, a good seal plus masking is the usual approach. For a constant low hum, ANC can help. For pure silence and nothing else, earplugs are hard to beat.

5. Battery, or no battery at all

Earplugs need no charging and never run flat, which is a real advantage. Wireless sleep earbuds trade that for sound, so check two numbers: how long the earbuds play on one charge, and how many extra charges the case holds. Many people only need sound while they fall asleep. A sleep timer that switches the audio off saves battery and means nothing keeps playing for the rest of the night.

If you would rather not have a radio on all night, look for low-energy Bluetooth and an automatic switch-off. SONSOH NIGHT plays for up to six hours per night, the case adds three full charges (about a week of nights), and the sleep timer powers everything down once you are asleep.

Try it at home

The pillow test, step by step.

01

Start with the medium tip

Insert the earbud with a gentle twist until outside sound turns muffled. That change is the seal, and without it neither blocking nor masking works well.

02

Lie down on your own pillow

Use the pillow you actually sleep on and lie on your usual side for at least five minutes. A firm pillow is a harder test than a soft one.

03

Press, then pay attention

Press your ear gently into the pillow. You should feel the pillow, not a hard edge. Pressure or pinching means a smaller tip or a flatter earbud.

04

Turn over twice

Roll to the other side and back again. If an earbud loosens, try the next size up for a firmer seal.

05

Give it a few nights

Anything new in your ear feels noticeable at first. Judge comfort after several nights, keep the volume as low as still covers the noise, and use the trial period if there is one.

FAQ

Side sleeper questions.

Which sleep earbuds are best for side sleepers? +

The ones you cannot feel on the pillow. Look for a flush fit with nothing sticking out of the ear, a low weight per earbud, several tip sizes and masking sound with a sleep timer. Then check them with the pillow test before you decide.

Can I sleep in my normal wireless earbuds? +

You can, but most everyday earbuds are made to be worn upright. Stems and larger housings stick out of the ear and press when you lie on them, and they usually have no sleep timer of their own. Back sleepers often get away with it; side sleepers tend to feel the pressure.

Earplugs or sleep earbuds for a snoring partner? +

Try earplugs first if silence works for you: they are cheap and need no battery. If you still hear the snoring through them, or silence itself keeps you awake, sleep earbuds add masking, a steady sound that makes the snoring stand out less.

How loud should sleep sounds be? +

As quiet as possible while still covering the noise that bothers you. A good seal lets you keep the volume lower, and a sleep timer stops playback once you are asleep.
